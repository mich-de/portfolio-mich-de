The Legal Risks of Enterprise AI Nobody Talks About: Privacy, GDPR, Vendors, and Best Practices (Giovanna Panucci)

Enterprise adoption of artificial intelligence is moving far faster than regulatory awareness. In an in-depth, practical interview on Raffaele Gaito's channel, attorney Giovanna Panucci — a specialist in privacy, personal data protection, and founder of the Gladiatori Digitali community — dismantles common myths and lays out an actionable playbook for using AI at work without exposing companies to data breaches, fines, or reputational damage.

The starting point is a mindset shift: traditional software was a "digital crutch" built for deterministic tasks, whereas AI learns and shifts beneath our feet. It is no longer a tool we lean on, but something we have to wear and tailor carefully.

1. Common Mistakes and the Shadow AI Threat

The most frequent mistake companies make is buying licenses on hype without first mapping out processes or use cases. That leads to two bad outcomes: either employees ignore the tools, or they overuse them recklessly by pasting internal documents, customer lists, and sensitive data directly into chatbots.

Even riskier is Shadow AI: when leadership bans AI tools or assumes nobody is using them, employees still turn to personal accounts in good faith, quietly punching holes in the company's security perimeter.

2. The Minimum Baseline for GDPR Compliance

To use language models professionally (whether in a corporation or as a solo consultant), Panucci outlines three non-negotiable rules:

  • No free or consumer plans: free tiers are strictly off-limits, and even paid consumer plans (like Plus or Pro) are inadequate for business. Simply toggling off model training is not enough, as it doesn't solve data residency or contractual safeguards. Businesses must use Business or Enterprise plans, which disable training by default and unlock Trust Centers for compliance documentation.
  • European data residency: always check where servers are physically located (for instance, enabling EU data residency on Enterprise tiers, or verifying that the provider adheres to the EU-US Data Privacy Framework).
  • Data Processing Agreement (GDPR Art. 28): formally designate the vendor as a data processor by downloading and executing their DPA. Once these safeguards are in place, handling standard personal data inside a compliant AI workspace becomes comparable to storing files in Google Workspace.

3. Chinese Models, Privacy-First Alternatives, and Prompt Hygiene

Regarding Chinese AI platforms (such as DeepSeek, Qwen, or agents like Manus), the recommended stance isn't an outright ban, but strict separation: they can be leveraged for external research and general analysis, provided zero personal data is ever uploaded.

For freelancers or anyone seeking an extra privacy layer, Duck.ai (by DuckDuckGo) is highlighted as a useful intermediary that strips user metadata (IP, browser fingerprint, location) before forwarding prompts and enforces deletion within 30 days.

Above all, prompt hygiene remains essential: before pasting a contract, email, or spreadsheet, anonymize it locally by replacing names, companies, and figures with placeholders (e.g., [full name]) using a simple Find and Replace before hitting send.

4. The Key Question for Vendors: System vs. Model

When buying turnkey AI software from a third-party vendor, one question eliminates 90% of governance headaches: "Do you own the underlying AI model?"

Most vendors today own only the system (the front-end, interface, and branding) while connecting via API to a third-party model (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google). Mapping the full chain of processors and sub-processors is critical because, in the event of a data breach, the data controller bears ultimate responsibility. A strong practical tip is to attach a data flow diagram directly to vendor contracts.

5. Internal Policies, Prompt Injection, and the AI Team

Putting guardrails in place doesn't require months of red tape. Small teams can start by updating their GDPR Art. 29 employee authorizations with a practical AI netiquette covering approved tools, placeholder usage, and how to flag hallucinations.

Larger organizations should add dedicated incident-response procedures for AI-specific threats — such as prompt injection attacks targeting corporate chatbots — and assemble a cross-functional AI Team combining IT, the DPO (Data Protection Officer), and an AI Strategist bridging business needs, market tools, and internal governance.

6. Intellectual Property and Copyright

Who owns an image generated with Midjourney or copy drafted with ChatGPT? While providers like OpenAI assign output rights to the user in their terms, that assignment only applies between you and the provider.

To claim actual copyright protection, human creative input must predominate (roughly exceeding 60-65%): writing a basic prompt does not establish authorship. By contrast, platforms like Adobe train exclusively on rights-cleared datasets (data cleansing), offering far stronger commercial safety from the ground up.

7. The "Tech Tower" and AI Act Obligations

To clarify responsibilities under the AI Act, Panucci breaks AI down into a four-layer stack: data, compute infrastructure, proprietary models, and end-user organizations (deployers).

If a company deploys third-party chatbots outside high-risk domains (Annex III of the AI Act covers high-risk uses like automated CV screening, education, and essential public services), the core requirement is transparency: clearly disclosing in client agreements, contracts, and website policies that AI is used to support workflows.

8. Processes Before Tools, Legal Prompting, and Human Oversight

The final part of the interview tackles "tool obsession": before buying licenses or chasing the latest autonomous agent ("buying a Ferrari to pick up bread"), companies should map internal workflows, pinpoint bottlenecks, and launch a handful of measurable pilot projects.

Operationally, Panucci shares the workflow that boosted her practice's efficiency by 75%: isolated project silos (with separate memory), a custom Tone of Voice document built via voice interview, and Legal Prompting — using precise wording and appending a simple anti-hallucination rule at the end of prompts: "if you are not 100% sure, say you don't know."

Her daily stack relies on a few focused tools (Claude for nuanced writing, ChatGPT for structured workflows, Perplexity for research, NotebookLM for strict source grounding on laws and legal commentary, Gamma for decks, and Manus as a web scout). Her closing image sums it up well: think of Iron Man — AI is the powered suit that amplifies what you can do, but a human must always remain inside, steering and supervising.

[ tip ]

Before uploading any business file to an AI tool, run a quick three-point check: is it a no-training Business/Enterprise tier? Do the data stay in Europe (or under a valid transfer framework)? And above all, have you already stripped personal data and replaced it with placeholders?
